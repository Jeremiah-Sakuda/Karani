# Municipal Broadband

Almost everything a person has to do now goes through the internet, applying for jobs, talking to a doctor, signing up for classes. Some cities have gotten tired of waiting and have started building broadband networks of their own, and there is a lot of arguing about whether that is something a city government should be doing.

The problem people usually point at is the price and the fact that in a lot of neighborhoods there is only one company selling service, so the price is whatever that company says it is. The city does not have to own the wires itself to fix this, it mostly needs to make sure somebody else is allowed to show up and compete, and there are a bunch of ways a council can do that without building anything.

Volkov looked at a city called Quarry Bend, which built a fiber network after years of complaints, and followed it for five years. The average residential bill in the covered area went from around seventy-four dollars a month down to around fifty-two, and by the third year a little over a third of the households in that area had signed up (Volkov 15).

When a network is treated as a piece of public equipment instead of a product to be sold, the questions the council asks about it change: not how much revenue can be pulled out of each household every month, but how many households are attached to it at all, and what that attachment costs the general fund. I think that is the right way to look at it and it is the part that convinced me (Volkov 21).

Aturo is more about the money side and how these projects get paid for. The builds that stayed healthy were the ones financed with revenue bonds that got paid back out of subscription income, while the ones that ran into trouble were the ones that had to come back to the council every single year and ask again, which turned the network into a political fight instead of a utility (Aturo, 2022, p. 158).

That second part matters. A network that has to win an argument every budget season makes short term decisions, and short term decisions in infrastructure are how you end up with something half built.

The obvious objection is cost. A mid sized city can put twenty or thirty million dollars into a build like this, and if not enough people sign up the debt does not go away, the taxpayers are on the hook for it whether they use the service or not.

I would answer that by saying cities already run complicated things. They run water and sewer and in some places the electric utility, none of that is simple either, and if the city needs engineers who understand fiber it can hire the same kind of engineers the private company hires, they are not a different species of person. Aturo's financing point covers a good amount of this too, since a revenue bond keeps the risk attached to the network instead of the general fund (Aturo 161).

This whole argument reminds me of the phone company situation. For most of the last century the phone system here was basically one company, the government eventually broke it into pieces, and people still go back and forth about whether that was worth doing. My grandfather says long distance got cheap almost overnight afterward and my mom says nobody could ever figure out who to call when something stopped working. I am not sure the breakup was the only way to get there, harder rules might have gotten a similar result, and a lot of those pieces merged back together later anyway. Anyway.

So my position is that cities should build these networks and they should run them themselves, not build the fiber and then lease it out to a private company to operate. Leasing it out puts the same kind of company back in charge of what the customer pays, so the city spends all that money and gives away the part of it that would have brought the price down, which is control. Owning the wire without owning the service is doing half the job and calling it finished.

Broadband is not a luxury anymore, it is closer to water than to cable television, and both of these studies point the same direction. Cities waiting for the market to fix this on its own have been waiting a long time, and in conclusion they should stop waiting.
