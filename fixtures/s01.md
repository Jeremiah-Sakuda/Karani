# What Counts as Infrastructure

We tend to settle the word *infrastructure* by appearance rather than by argument, picturing poured concrete, buried pipe, and steel towers, and assigning the label to whatever looks sufficiently heavy. A functional test serves better. A facility belongs to infrastructure when three conditions hold at once: the fixed cost of building it dwarfs the cost of serving one additional user, its value to each user rises as more of the community is connected to it, and duplicating it street by street wastes more than the resulting competition returns. Water distribution passes this test, as do the electrical grid, the sanitary sewer, and the street itself. Broadband passes it too, and passes cleanly, which suggests that the question of who should own the last mile is not a new question but an old one wearing newer equipment.

My position is that a city served by a single wireline provider should build and operate its own broadband network, and that the burden of justification belongs to those who would leave that facility in private hands rather than to the council proposing to take it up. That is a stronger claim than the usual one, which treats a municipal build as an emergency measure available only after the private market has visibly failed, and I want to defend the stronger version.

The economics are not in serious dispute. Aberdene, surveying construction costs across mid-sized systems, places the trenched cost of fiber in an established residential grid between forty and seventy thousand dollars per mile, while the cost of attaching one more household to a line already passing the property is what he calls "effectively rounding error" (11). A cost structure of that shape does not produce rivalry; it produces one wire. A second entrant must pay the entire fixed cost of construction in order to compete for a fraction of the households the first entrant already serves, and no board approves that arithmetic twice in the same neighborhood. Aberdene's more useful point comes later, when he observes that regulating a private owner of such a facility requires the regulator to know the firm's costs at least as well as the firm does, a condition that is never satisfied and rarely even approximated (14-15).

If competition cannot discipline the last mile and regulation can only guess at it, the remaining option is ownership, which relocates the decision to a body whose surplus returns to the people paying the bills. The objection that this is ideological rather than practical has the matter backwards: the ideology is in assuming that a facility with these properties becomes a market because we call it one.

Castellanos supplies the evidence that public ownership can actually be executed. Tracking twenty-three municipal systems in cities of comparable size across a twelve-year window, she sorts them not by region or demographics but by governance structure, and the sort turns out to be the one that predicts outcomes. Seventeen of the twenty-three met their debt service obligations by the fourth year of operation, and the systems that met them early shared three features: an independent utility board insulated from the council's election cycle, revenue bonds ring-fenced from the general fund, and rates set by the board rather than negotiated annually in public session (Castellanos 206-07). The systems that struggled were not the ones in the poorest cities. They were the ones whose rates became a campaign issue, and whose boards therefore could not raise them.

The strongest objection to my position is not that cities are incompetent, and not that government should stay out of enterprise. It is that broadband is unlike water in the one respect that matters most to the definitional argument I opened with: water mains are not made obsolete by a new standard, and fiber networks arguably are. A city that issues thirty-year bonds against a plant whose working life is closer to seven has mismatched the debt to the asset, and will find itself re-equipping a system it has not finished paying for. This objection attacks the analogy at its foundation rather than quibbling with the cost figures, which is why it deserves the space I am giving it.

The answer is to stop treating the network as one asset. Castellanos separates first cost into placement, passive plant, and active electronics, and finds that conduit and the labor of putting it in the ground account for the large majority of capital, with the electronics at each end closer to a seventh of the total (208). The perishable layer is the cheap layer. Conduit installed today will accept strands that have not been manufactured yet, and the same strand has already carried several generations of transmission standards without being disturbed. A city that bonds the trench over thirty years and depreciates the electronics over seven is doing nothing more exotic than what electric utilities have done with substations and meters for a century. The objection does establish one real constraint, which is that a municipality should not advertise a rate premised on never re-equipping the system, and the failures Castellanos documents include at least two cities that did exactly that.

I should be careful about what I have and have not argued. I have not argued that every municipality should build, and the conditions I would attach are specific. The argument applies where one wireline provider serves the entire footprint, where the city already owns pole attachments or operates an electric utility with crews who know how to open a street, and where the system can be governed at genuine arm's length from the body that authorizes it. Where those conditions hold, the network is eligible for public ownership on precisely the grounds that the water system is, and in a town that long ago decided its water should not be sold to it at whatever the market would bear, eligibility is most of the argument.
