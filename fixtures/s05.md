# A Taxonomy of Municipal Suitability: Toward a Population-Contingent Evaluation of Public Broadband Provision

The question of whether broadband infrastructure ought to be constructed and operated by municipal governments has been the occasion of considerable disputation, and that disputation has been characterized, on both sides of it, by a regrettable tendency toward overgeneralization. Proponents of public provision speak of "cities" as though the category were a homogeneous one; opponents of public provision are guilty of the identical procedure. It is the contention of this essay that such generalization constitutes the principal defect of the present debate. The determinative consideration is not an ideological consideration but a structural one, and the structural variable possessing the greatest explanatory power is population. Accordingly, it is submitted that municipal construction and operation of broadband networks is warranted in municipalities of substantial population, is contraindicated in municipalities of small population, and is, in the intermediate case, contingent upon conditions which require individual assessment rather than presumption. What follows is an attempt at the establishment of a taxonomy by which such assessment may be conducted.

Three categories are proposed. Category I comprises municipalities of fewer than twenty-five thousand residents; Category II, those of between twenty-five thousand and one hundred fifty thousand; Category III, those in excess of one hundred fifty thousand. No claim of precision is advanced on behalf of these thresholds, which are offered as approximations. What is claimed is that the differences between the categories are differences of kind rather than of degree, in that the mechanisms by which a public network succeeds or fails are not the same mechanisms in all three instances. Consideration of Category II is deferred to the conclusion of the analysis, the intermediate case being most intelligibly understood by reference to the extremes by which it is bounded.

With respect to Category I, opposition is warranted, and the grounds of opposition are three in number and cumulative in their operation. The first is a matter of fixed cost. The expense attending the construction of a fiber plant does not diminish in proportion to the smallness of the population across which it must be amortized, with the consequence that the per-subscriber debt burden is of a quantum not encountered in larger jurisdictions. The second ground is administrative in character. Grimaldi's examination of small public systems establishes that the retention of qualified technical personnel constitutes a persistent difficulty for governments whose entire employment establishment may number in the dozens, and that the departure of a single such individual has eventuated in degradations of service from which recovery was protracted (Grimaldi 233-49). The third ground is one of risk absorption. A municipality of eight thousand residents possesses no reserve against a year of underperformance, and a shortfall which a larger government would absorb as an accounting adjustment becomes an emergency necessitating the reallocation of committed funds. One does not wish to be understood as asserting that small municipalities are governed less competently than large ones. What is asserted is that the consequences of ordinary error are, in their case, disproportionate to the error.

With respect to Category III, by contrast, the case for public provision is a strong one, and it is strong for reasons which are the inverse of the foregoing. Density is the operative advantage. Where residences and commercial premises are situated in proximity, the cost of construction per premise passed is substantially reduced, and Oyelaran's treatment of urban cost structure demonstrates that the relationship between density and per-premise expenditure is not a linear one but one in which the advantages accrue disproportionately to the densest jurisdictions. (Oyelaran 55-61) A further advantage lies in institutional demand, since such governments are themselves substantial consumers of connectivity, on account of the requirements of public safety communications and school administration, and the internalization of expenditures presently made to external vendors furnishes a revenue foundation not dependent upon residential adoption at all. To this must be added the superior borrowing terms available to larger issuers.

The opponent of the position here advanced has available to him an objection of the following description: that the taxonomy produces a perverse result. The municipalities in which private provision is least adequate are precisely the small and remote ones, and a framework which withholds the remedy from the jurisdictions suffering the affliction most acutely, while extending it to jurisdictions already possessed of multiple providers, may be thought to have inverted the priorities of the problem. No denial of the difficulty so described is here offered. The response available is that the unit of provision need not be identical with the unit of government. Aggregation at the county level permits the fixed costs of construction and, more importantly, of technical staffing to be distributed across a subscriber base of adequate size, and Grimaldi's discussion of cooperative arrangements indicates that the systems exhibiting the greatest durability among smaller communities have been those organized regionally rather than municipally (Grimaldi 233-49). It must be conceded that this response is a partial one only. The political apparatus by which aggregation is accomplished is itself frequently absent where the need is greatest, and the objection therefore retains a residue which the present framework does not dissolve.

There remains Category II. Here no general determination is possible, and the honest conclusion is that suitability is conditional. Three conditions are proposed. The first is the pre-existence of a municipal electric utility, since the billing infrastructure, the pole attachments, and the operational culture of such a utility constitute the greater part of what a broadband undertaking requires. The second is municipal ownership of conduit already in the ground, the absence of which converts the enterprise into an excavation project of indeterminate expense. The third is a debt structure under which repayment is pledged against the revenues of the system rather than against general municipal revenues, so that failure, should it occur, is not permitted to propagate into unrelated services. Where all three conditions are satisfied the undertaking is defensible; where two are satisfied caution is indicated; where fewer than two are satisfied, the difficulties which attend such an undertaking is sufficient to counsel against it.

The conclusion to be drawn is that the question posed by the assignment does not admit of a single answer, and that the demand for a single answer is itself the error. Municipal broadband is an instrument, and the suitability of an instrument is determined by the dimensions of the object to which it is applied.
