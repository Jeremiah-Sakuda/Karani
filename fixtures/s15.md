# The Right to Be Reachable

Every generation inherits the same question about what belongs to everybody and what belongs to whoever can afford it, and every generation gets it wrong for a while before it gets it right, we got it wrong about roads, we got it wrong about electricity, we got it wrong about the mail until somebody decided a letter to a farmhouse was worth the same three cents as a letter across town. Right now we are getting it wrong about the internet. City councils think they are debating a technical matter of fiber and bond issues and cost recovery, but that is not the real debate, the real debate is whether a person living in this country has a right to be connected to the world or merely a revocable privilege administered by a billing department. I say it is a right. Cities should build and operate their own networks, because a right that obligates nobody in particular to provide anything in particular is not a right at all, it is a slogan with good manners.

Start with what a right actually is, since the word gets flung around so casually that it has nearly stopped meaning anything. A right is not a preference and it is not an aspiration, it is a claim strong enough to generate a duty in somebody else. If I have a right to counsel I have not merely expressed enthusiasm for legal representation, somebody is obligated to show up. That is the entire architecture of the concept, and it is why the word carries weight. So when we say, as we now say constantly and thoughtlessly, that internet access is essential in modern life, we are either saying something with teeth in it or we are making pleasant noise at a public meeting.

The scholarship has finally caught up to what ordinary people figured out years ago. Ellery's study of connectivity and civic participation settles the matter decisively, she establishes that internet access already qualifies as a fundamental human right and that everything still unresolved is merely administrative. Her language leaves no wiggle room: broadband access "has become a precondition for meaningful participation in civic and economic life, though the question of which institutions bear the duty to supply it remains genuinely open" (Ellery 94). Precondition. Not amenity, not indulgence, not a pleasant extra to be distributed when the market feels charitable that quarter. Once you have conceded that a good is a precondition for participating in the society that governs you, you have conceded the obligation along with it, the remainder is paperwork and courage.

Dabrowski supplies the ground floor of fact. Surveying households across seven municipalities, he found that thirty-four percent of lower-income households in one carrier's service territory had either no home connection at all or one too degraded to carry a video class without collapsing (Dabrowski 253). He also documents what happens when a city takes the job itself, coverage in the municipality of Hesperton went from patchy and arbitrary to functionally universal inside three years, precisely because the city was never required to justify the profitability of a particular block to anyone (Dabrowski 256). A city answers to residents. That difference is not cosmetic, it is the whole point.

A right is easiest to recognize when you watch it being denied. Consider what it takes to file for unemployment insurance in most states now, the phone lines are largely decorative, the paper form is a rumor, the actual door into the building is a web portal that times out after ninety seconds. Consider a diabetic whose insulin pump reports to an application. Consider a ninth grader whose homework, textbook, instructor and grade all live on a server somewhere. We constructed an entire society on the assumption of connection and then left the connecting to firms whose duty runs to their investors rather than to the ninth grader.

The strongest thing said against municipal networks is that city government has no business competing with private industry, and that when the venture underperforms it is taxpayers who are left holding the bag. I understand the anxiety. But look at what the objection quietly assumes, it assumes broadband is a business first and a necessity second, which is exactly the premise under dispute. Nobody proposes that the fire department withdraw from firefighting because a subscription brigade might undercut it. And if the concern is genuinely about money then the concern actually argues my side, because the public is already paying the cost of exclusion, it simply pays it in a currency that never appears on a municipal balance sheet, in students who quietly fall two years behind, in adults who cannot submit applications, in clinics that abandon remote appointments, in benefits offices forced to staff phone banks for the very people the portal locked out, and that bill comes due decades later in the form of a poorer and smaller city (Dabrowski 257).

There is a second objection, which is that cities are not good at technology. Some of them are not. Cities are also mediocre at plowing snow and they plow it anyway, incompetence is an argument for hiring better people, it is not an argument for abandoning a duty you have already acknowledged.

So build them. Build them the way we once strung wire down county roads that no rational investor would have wired, because the alternative was telling a whole category of citizen that the century did not include them. Municipal broadband is simply what the right to be reachable looks like once it stops being a sentiment expressed at podiums and becomes an institution with a budget and an address. Ellery and Dabrowski both point the same direction. The obligation exists. The only remaining question is whether we intend to honor it or keep admiring it.
